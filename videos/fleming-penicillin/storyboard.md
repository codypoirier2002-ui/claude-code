# Fleming's moldy dish → penicillin (1928) — storyboard
Artist's reenactment (stylized cartoon figures; no dialogue or private details invented).

## Verified facts (sources opened)
- Returning from holiday on **September 3, 1928**, Fleming (Professor of Bacteriology, St Mary's Hospital, London) sorted through petri dishes of *Staphylococcus*. One dish had a blob of mold; the zone around it was clear. Mold later identified as a rare strain of *Penicillium notatum*. — ACS Chemical Landmark, https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/flemingpenicillin.html
- His "mold juice" killed streptococcus, meningococcus, diphtheria bacillus. Crude and very unstable; published June 1929 (British Journal of Experimental Pathology) with only a passing mention of therapeutic use. — ACS (same page)
- Fleming's own words: plate contaminated by a mould; staphylococcal colonies near it "had become translucent" and lysis was going on; mould identified as *Penicillium*. — Nobel Lecture, 11 Dec 1945, https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1945/fleming/lecture/ (PDF https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fleming-lecture.pdf)
- Florey, Chain and the Oxford team (Dunn School) turned it into a drug: mold grown in baths, bedpans, milk churns, food tins; first patient Albert Alexander, 12 Feb 1941 (improved, then supplies ran out). — ACS
- 1945: Fleming, Florey and Chain awarded the Nobel Prize for penicillin research. — ACS; Nobel lecture ("my co-participators in this Nobel Award").
- Myth avoided: no claim about an open window / where the spore came from (not established); Fleming did not turn it into a medicine himself.
